Almost everyone assumes buffering means their internet is too slow. It usually
does not. A 4K stream needs roughly 25 Mbps and a good HD stream around 8 Mbps,
and most US home connections are comfortably past both. If yours is, speed is
not your problem — something between the router and the screen is.

Work through these in order. The first three take under a minute and solve most
cases.

## 1. Check whether it is one channel or all of them

This single question splits the problem in half, so do it first.

If **one channel** stutters and the rest are fine, nothing on your end is
broken. That channel's source is having a moment. Sports channels do this during
big fixtures, when far more people are watching at once. Try it again later, or
try a different feed of the same channel if there is one.

If **everything** stutters, carry on down this list.

## 2. Move the stick away from the back of the TV

This sounds too simple to matter and it is the single most common fix we give.

A Firestick or similar plugged directly into an HDMI port sits in a slot
surrounded by the television's metal casing and its own electronics. Both block
Wi-Fi. The stick reports a decent signal and still cannot hold a steady stream.

Use the short HDMI extender that came in the box — that is exactly what it is
for — so the stick hangs clear of the panel. People are routinely surprised by
how much this alone changes.

## 3. Restart the router, not just the app

Reopening the app clears very little. Unplug the router, wait thirty seconds,
plug it back in and let it come up fully.

Home routers accumulate state and drop quality slowly over days. A restart is
not superstition here; it genuinely resets the radio and the connection table.
If your evenings are reliably worse than your mornings, this is often why.

## 4. Look at what else is on your Wi-Fi

Streaming shares the connection with everything else in the house. A large game
download, a cloud backup running at night, or four other people on video calls
will all take their share.

Check what is downloading before you assume the service is at fault. Game
consoles in particular update in the background without telling anyone.

## 5. Use 5 GHz, or better, use a cable

Most routers broadcast two networks. The 2.4 GHz one reaches further and is
slower and far more congested — it is competing with your neighbours, your
microwave and every smart plug in the house. The 5 GHz one is faster and much
cleaner over shorter distances.

If your network names look like `Home` and `Home_5G`, connect the streaming
device to the second one.

Better still, run Ethernet if the device supports it. A wired connection removes
the entire category of problem. For a Firestick this means a small USB Ethernet
adapter; on an Android box or a smart TV there is usually a port on the back
already.

## 6. Try a different player app

Players are not equal. Some buffer more aggressively, some handle a wobbly
connection far better than others. If everything else looks healthy, installing
a different player and using the same login details is a fast way to find out
whether the app was the problem.

## 7. Now check your speed — properly

If you have reached this point, test your connection — but test it **on the
device you are streaming with**, in the place where it lives.

A speed test on your phone standing next to the router tells you about your
phone standing next to the router. The number that matters is what the stick
gets in the corner of the living room where it actually sits, and the two are
often very different.
